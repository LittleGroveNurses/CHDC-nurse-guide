Why the child health check matters

The CHDC is not about finding problems — it is about understanding the whole child within their family and environment, and identifying difficulties early when intervention is most effective. Brain plasticity is greatest in the first five years.

Developmental surveillance

Surveillance is an ongoing, flexible process — not a single test. It involves listening to parent concerns, observing the child, and comparing what you see against expected developmental ranges. Always ask about parent concerns first; they are your most important source of information.

Sheridan's six developmental domains

No domain develops in isolation — delays in one area often affect others.

Gross motor
Large body movements — balance, coordination, walking, running, climbing
Fine motor
Hand and finger skills — grasping, drawing, manipulating objects
Speech & language
Expressive and receptive language — words, sentences, understanding
Social & emotional
Relationships, play, self-regulation, emotional expression
Cognitive
Problem-solving, learning, memory, cause and effect
Vision & hearing
Sensory foundations that underpin all other development
Little Grove's role

Little Grove delivers CHDCs in clinic (Underdale and Thebarton) and in ECEC settings. You use structured tools — ASQ-3, ASQ:SE-2, Brigance — alongside direct observation and parent report. The CHDC app is your real-time clinical reference; keep it updated and out of family view.

Growth assessment

📱 CHDC App: Growth — Weight, Height/Length, BMI, Head Circumference. Growth monitoring is one of the most sensitive indicators of a child's overall health. Serial measurements are more informative than single readings.

Weight & height / length
What to look forWhy it matters
Weigh undressed (nappy only for infants). Use calibrated scales.Clothing adds 0.5–1 kg; inconsistent technique inflates measurement error across visits.
Plot on correct WHO chart (0–2 yr) or CDC chart (2 yr+) for age and sex.Charts differ significantly; using the wrong chart produces misleading percentile results.
Note trend across visits — crossing two or more centile lines downward is more significant than a single low reading.Faltering growth is defined by sustained downward centile crossing, not a single low weight.
Length (lying) for under 2 years; standing height (stadiometer) for 2 years and over.Mixing techniques introduces up to 2 cm discrepancy, distorting height-for-age percentile.
✓ Weight and height/length tracking within ±2 centile lines of previous visit.
✓ Growth pattern consistent with parental heights (mid-parental height calculation).
● Weight crossing two or more major centile lines downward over two or more visits.
● Weight consistently below 2nd percentile with no familial explanation.
● Disproportionate upward weight gain — consider dietary review.
▲ Sustained weight faltering unresponsive to dietary and feeding intervention
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: dietitian review, then paediatric review if no response. Specialist: Paediatric dietitian / general paediatrician.
▲ Height below 0.4th percentile with no familial cause
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: paediatric review. Specialist: General paediatrician or paediatric endocrinologist.
▲ Severe acute malnutrition (weight-for-height Z-score < −3)
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: same-day paediatric review. Specialist: General paediatrician / ED if acutely unwell.
BMI — 2 years and older only

BMI is a screening tool only. In children, BMI is age- and sex-specific (BMI-for-age percentile).

BMI-for-age percentileInterpretation & action
< 5th percentileUnderweight — assess dietary intake, psychosocial factors, underlying illness.
5th–84th percentileHealthy weight range.
85th–94th percentileOverweight — dietary and activity counselling; monitor at next check.
≥ 95th percentileObesity — structured management plan; refer to NP for dietitian referral and monitoring.
Confidence note: BMI cut-off percentiles vary slightly between Australian NHMRC and IOTF thresholds. Always cross-reference with current SA Health or NHMRC guidance.
Head circumference — 6 month, 18 month, 2 year checks only

Measure with a non-stretch tape at the maximum occipitofrontal circumference. Plot on the WHO chart.

What to look forWhy it matters
Consistent technique — tape above eyebrows, around widest part.Inconsistent technique produces up to 1 cm error, significant on a growth chart.
Note fontanelle size and tension at 6 months.Bulging fontanelle with rapidly growing head circumference suggests raised intracranial pressure.
Downward centile crossing over serial measurements.May indicate poor brain growth; requires investigation.
▲ Head circumference crossing ≥2 centile lines upward (macrocephaly)
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: paediatric review; imaging may be indicated. Specialist: General paediatrician — rule out hydrocephalus.
▲ Head circumference crossing ≥2 centile lines downward (microcephaly)
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: paediatric review. Specialist: General paediatrician or paediatric neurologist.
▲ Any sudden change in rate of head circumference growth
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: same-day paediatric review. Specialist: General paediatrician.

Eyes & vision

📱 CHDC App: Eyes — Vision Screening, Cover Test, Corneal Reflections. Vision screening aims to detect amblyopia and its causes during the critical window of visual development (birth to ~7–8 years). Untreated amblyopia causes permanent visual impairment.

Observation — all ages
What to look forWhy it matters
Observe eyes at rest and during play before formal testing.Squinting, head tilting, eye rubbing, or avoiding one eye being covered may reveal concerns in uncooperative children.
Note structural abnormalities: ptosis, coloboma, nystagmus, corneal clouding.May indicate underlying ophthalmic or systemic disease requiring specialist review.
▲ Leukocoria — white pupil reflex
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: same-day urgent referral — discuss with Ariane immediately. Specialist: Ophthalmology same day. May indicate retinoblastoma or cataract.
Corneal light reflections (Hirschberg test) — all ages

Shine a pen torch at the bridge of the nose from ~50 cm. Symmetrical central reflections indicate normal ocular alignment. Asymmetry suggests strabismus.

Pseudostrabismus is common in infants with a flat nasal bridge — eyes appear crossed but corneal reflections are symmetrical. This is normal and resolves as the face matures.
▲ Asymmetric corneal light reflex at any age
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: orthoptist assessment. Specialist: Orthoptist, then ophthalmologist if confirmed.
Fixation & cover test — all ages
What to look forWhy it matters
Does the child fixate on a target and follow it smoothly?Failure to fixate by 6–8 weeks or follow by 3–4 months is a red flag for significant visual impairment.
Cover each eye alternately — note if child objects more to one side, or if eye deviates when uncovered.Unequal objection suggests amblyopia. Refixation on the cover-uncover test indicates strabismus.
▲ Failure to fixate or follow by 3–4 months
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: urgent referral. Specialist: Paediatric ophthalmologist — urgent.
▲ Confirmed strabismus (refixation on cover test)
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: orthoptist assessment. Specialist: Orthoptist / ophthalmologist.
Vision chart — 4 year check only

Formal visual acuity at 3 metres using an age-appropriate chart (Kay Pictures, HOTV, or Lea Symbols). Test each eye separately with fellow eye fully occluded. Record as 6/__ for each eye.

6/6 – 6/9
Normal for age. No action required.
6/12 one eye
Borderline — retest at second visit before referring.
6/18 or worse
Or ≥2-line interocular difference — refer to NP.
▲ Visual acuity 6/18 or worse, or ≥2-line interocular difference
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: orthoptist review. Specialist: Orthoptist, then ophthalmologist as indicated.
▲ Visual acuity 6/12 persisting at second visit
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: orthoptist review. Specialist: Orthoptist.
Testing conditions: Ensure adequate lighting, correct 3 m distance, and full occlusion of the fellow eye. Note head tilting or squinting even if the child achieves 6/9.

Ear health & hearing

📱 CHDC App: Hearing — Otoscopy, Sound Scouts (4 year). Hearing loss is the most common sensory impairment in childhood. Conductive hearing loss from OME is highly prevalent and has significant consequences for language and learning if missed.

Ear history — all ages
What to askWhy it matters
Number of episodes of acute otitis media (AOM) in past 12 months.≥3 episodes in 6 months or ≥4 in 12 months = recurrent AOM — specialist review warranted.
Persistent or intermittent rhinorrhoea — duration, colour, frequency.Chronic nasal congestion is associated with Eustachian tube dysfunction and OME, contributing to conductive hearing loss.
Tympanic membrane examination — all ages

A normal TM is pearly grey, translucent, with a well-defined cone of light and visible bony landmarks. Describe rather than just ticking Normal/Abnormal.

FindingWhat it means
Dull, opaque, amber or blue TM; loss of landmarks; decreased mobilityOME ('glue ear') — fluid behind drum without acute infection.
Bulging red/yellow TM, loss of landmarks, restricted mobilityAcute otitis media — assess for antibiotic indication per current Therapeutic Guidelines.
Perforation — irregular margin, visible middle ear structuresDocument wet (active) or dry. Refer ENT regardless.
Not sightedWax occlusion most common — document reason. Refer if hearing concern present.
Diagnosing AOM correctly: AOM requires all three of the following: (1) acute onset of symptoms; (2) abnormal tympanic membrane examination with signs of middle ear effusion (MEE); and (3) signs of middle ear inflammation (bulging TM, erythema, or otalgia). A red TM alone does not diagnose AOM — the most common cause of a red TM in a child is crying or a viral URTI. Most AOM episodes resolve spontaneously. Routine antibiotics are not recommended for uncomplicated AOM in children aged ≥2 years — use watchful waiting unless systemically unwell, immunocompromised, or bilaterally affected with severe symptoms. Refer to the RCH Clinical Practice Guideline (updated February 2025) and use the AOM management flowchart for antibiotic decision-making.
TM appearances — RCH image reference: Normal TM · Injected TM · Bulging/AOM · OME · Perforation · Otitis externa. Images available via the RCH Acute Otitis Media CPG (PIC Endorsed, last updated June 2021).
AOM management flowchart: View RCH AOM management flowchart (February 2025)
✓ Pearly grey, translucent TM with visible cone of light and bony landmarks.
✓ No rhinorrhoea or rhinorrhoea consistent with brief viral URTI.
✓ No report of recurrent ear infections.
▲ Recurrent AOM — ≥3 in 6 months or ≥4 in 12 months
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: ENT referral via NP letter. Specialist: ENT surgeon.
▲ OME persisting beyond 3 months with language delay or hearing concern
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: ENT referral via NP letter. Specialist: ENT surgeon.
▲ TM perforation
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: ENT referral — not necessarily urgent unless wet/symptomatic. Specialist: ENT surgeon.
▲ Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander child with OME
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: lower threshold — ENT referral per NHMRC Ear Health guidelines. Specialist: ENT surgeon.
Sound Scouts — 4 year check only

Sound Scouts is a validated app-based pure-tone hearing screener for children aged 4–17 years, used with calibrated over-ear headphones. Login uses the SSIMPL clinic credentials (obtain from Ariane).

Before every test: Confirm the correct calibrated headphones are selected in the app. Results from uncalibrated equipment are unreliable.

Initial test — step by step

  • 1
    Login
    Open Sound Scouts app. Tap Settings → Login. Enter SSIMPL User ID and password.
  • 2
    Create player
    Tap New Session → Start → I Agree → Create Player. Enter child's details, tap Submit → Test This Player.
  • 3
    Select Automatic Audiometer
    Choose Automatic Audiometer (not Triple Factor Hearing Screener). Tap Begin Test.
  • 4
    Select standardised equipment
    Choose the correct headphones from the list — mandatory. Do not proceed without selecting equipment.
  • 5
    Tone selection
    Select Standard tab. Default tests both ears at 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz to 20 dB HL. You can test one ear at a time if fatigue or focus is an issue.
  • 6
    Headphone sound check
    Tap Play and select the word you hear in each ear (Icecream / Meatpie / Popcorn). Ensure headphones are placed correctly — mark the right ear if helpful.
  • 7
    Explain the task to the child
    "You'll hear some beeps — tap the circle every time you hear one, even if it sounds very quiet. The beeps get quieter and quieter — that's normal." Single Circle Response mode is suitable for most children.
  • 8
    Trial → Begin Test
    Select Start Trial to try both modes. Choose preferred mode using button on the right. Tap Begin Test.
  • 9
    Review results
    Results show Pass or Refer per ear, Hearing Number, and audiogram. Red = right ear, blue = left ear. Check bottom of report for any Invalid/Unreliable frequencies.

Interpreting results

Pass
Both ears pass at 20 dB HL. No action required.
Incomplete / N/A
Document reason. Reschedule or refer to NP if not completable at second attempt.
Refer
One or both ears fail. Refer to NP — typical path is audiology. Do not re-screen; refer directly.

Retest protocol — if frequency flagged Invalid/Unreliable

  • 1
    Identify what needs retesting
    Check report for which ear (red = right, blue = left) and which frequency (500, 1000, 2000, or 4000 Hz) is flagged.
  • 2
    Start a new test for the same player
    Tap New Session → Start → I Agree → Player's Name → Test This Player. Select Automatic Audiometer → Standardised Equipment.
  • 3
    Update or Add New
    If a recent test is detected, select Update to produce one consolidated report. Select Add New only if you need a separate record.
  • 4
    Tone selection — specific frequencies only
    Standard tab. Uncheck all frequencies except the one(s) to be retested. Select the specific ear. If both ears need retesting, complete each ear as a separate run.
  • 5
    Complete check, trial, and test
    Follow same steps as initial test. Use the same test mode as the original session.
  • 6
    Report updates automatically
    If Update was selected, the report incorporates new results. Review the updated audiogram and document the final result.
▲ Sound Scouts Refer — confirmed after retest if applicable
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: formal audiology referral via NP. Specialist: Audiologist (paediatric). Do not repeat screening beyond one retest.
▲ Sound Scouts Incomplete at two attempts with hearing concern
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: audiology referral via NP. Specialist: Audiologist (paediatric).

Oral health — 'Lift the Lip'

📱 CHDC App: Oral Health — Dental Caries Screening. Early childhood caries is largely preventable. The Lift the Lip technique enables detection at the earliest stage. Used at every Little Grove check from 6 months.

Technique

With the child settled, lift the upper lip firmly to expose the labial surface of the upper incisors and gum line. Use a light source. Examine lower anterior teeth also if possible.

What to look for
FindingWhy it matters
Visible plaque — white or yellow deposits at the gum line.Plaque is the precursor to caries. Indicates need for dietary and oral hygiene counselling and possible dental referral.
White spot lesions — chalky, opaque areas on enamel, typically cervical.Early carious lesions — demineralisation without cavitation. Remineralisation possible with fluoride and diet change.
Brown cavitation or frank caries.Active decay requiring dental treatment. Refer promptly.
Bleeding or swollen gums (18 months+).Gingivitis — typically from inadequate brushing. Counsel on technique. Persistent bleeding warrants dental review.
Facial swelling — 4 year check.May indicate dental abscess — urgent dental review or ED if systemic signs present.
✓ Erupted teeth clean, no visible plaque, pink healthy gingiva.
✓ Parent reports twice-daily brushing with age-appropriate fluoride toothpaste.
▲ Any white spot lesion
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: dental referral via NP. Specialist: SA Dental Service or private dentist.
▲ Frank caries or cavitation at any age
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: urgent dental referral via NP. Specialist: SA Dental Service or private dentist — same week.
▲ Facial swelling with dental pain
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: refer to NP same day. Specialist: Dentist same day; ED if systemically unwell — risk of cellulitis or sepsis.
▲ Persistent plaque with non-engagement from family
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: dental referral via NP. Specialist: Community dental service for family support.

Immunisation

📱 CHDC App: Immunisation — NIP Schedule. At Little Grove checks, you are verifying coverage and identifying gaps — not administering vaccines. The Australian Immunisation Register (AIR) is the authoritative record; access via PRODA/HPOS or ask the parent to show the AIR history statement via myGov.

What to check
What to look forWhy it matters
Is immunisation up to date per the NIP schedule for this child's age?On-time vaccination protects the child and the population. Delays increase vulnerability, particularly for pertussis and measles.
Parental concerns, conscientious objection, or medical contraindications?Understanding the reason guides follow-up. Contraindications must be documented. Conscientious objection has legal and funding implications.

Physical examination

📱 CHDC App: Physical Examination — Age-Specific Findings. This section covers all age-specific and all-ages physical examination items from the Little Grove CHDC form.

6 month — head shape & fontanelle
What to look forWhy it matters
Anterior fontanelle — size, tension, and closure.Typically closes 9–18 months. Bulging at rest (not crying) suggests raised intracranial pressure. Sunken suggests dehydration. Premature closure may indicate craniosynostosis.
Head shape — plagiocephaly, dolichocephaly, brachycephaly.Positional plagiocephaly is very common and benign. Distinguish from craniosynostosis by assessing ear displacement on the flat side and checking for sutural ridging.
▲ Bulging or tense fontanelle at rest
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: same-day medical review. Specialist: General paediatrician or ED if acutely unwell.
▲ Craniosynostosis suspected — sutural ridging, ear displacement
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: paediatric review. Specialist: Craniofacial service.
6 month — hip abduction
What to look forWhy it matters
With infant supine, hips and knees flexed to 90°, abduct each hip. Both should abduct symmetrically to ~80°.Restriction or asymmetry may indicate developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH). The 6-month check is the last routine opportunity for clinical DDH screening before weight-bearing.
Note clicks, clunks, or resistance on abduction.A 'clunk' (Ortolani sign) indicates hip reduction — refer urgently. More significant than a 'click'.
Technique video: Hip abduction examination technique (YouTube)
▲ Restricted or asymmetric hip abduction
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: paediatric orthopaedics referral via NP — ultrasound or X-ray may be indicated. Specialist: Paediatric orthopaedic surgeon.
6 month — genitalia & inguinal area
What to look forWhy it matters
Inspect genitalia — normal anatomical appearance for sex.Ambiguous genitalia requires urgent specialist review. Document respectfully; this is a sensitive examination.
Inguinal area — any masses or asymmetry?Inguinal hernia presents as an intermittent or persistent groin swelling, more common in males and preterm infants. Incarceration risk.
▲ Ambiguous genitalia
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: urgent paediatric review via NP. Specialist: Paediatric endocrinology or urology — urgent.
▲ Inguinal hernia — groin mass intermittent or persistent
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: paediatric surgery referral via NP. Specialist: Paediatric surgeon.
All ages — testes
What to look forWhy it matters
Palpate for testes bilaterally. Note position — fully descended, retractile, or undescended.Cryptorchidism occurs in ~1% of term males at one year. Spontaneous descent after 6 months is unlikely. Orchidopexy before 18 months preserves fertility and reduces malignancy risk.
▲ Undescended testis not previously referred
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: paediatric surgery referral via NP. Specialist: Paediatric surgeon — orchidopexy ideally before 18 months.
▲ Testicular mass, sudden scrotal pain, or significant asymmetry
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: same-day review via NP. Specialist: ED or paediatric surgery — urgent.
All ages — skin
What to look forWhy it matters
Inspect skin — colour, rashes, lesions, turgor, birthmarks.Mongolian spots: benign, document to prevent confusion with bruising. Café-au-lait spots: >6 or >1.5 cm diameter may indicate neurofibromatosis. Haemangiomas: most involute; note if ulcerating or near eye/airway.
Unexplained bruising — especially in pre-mobile infants.Bruising in a child not yet weight-bearing is a significant child protection concern. Document location, shape, colour, and number. Discuss with Ariane immediately.
Child protection: Bruising in a pre-mobile infant must be discussed with Ariane before the family leaves. Mandatory reporting obligations may apply — see Safeguarding section.
All ages — chest & heart sounds
What to look forWhy it matters
Auscultate lung fields bilaterally — equal air entry, absence of added sounds.Wheeze: bronchiolitis (infants), asthma (older children), or foreign body. Crackles suggest lower respiratory tract infection.
Breath sounds reference video: Paediatric breath sounds guide (YouTube)
Auscultate heart sounds — rate, rhythm, any murmur?Innocent murmurs are common (Still's murmur, pulmonary flow murmur). Features suggesting pathological murmur: pansystolic, harsh, grade ≥3/6, associated thrill, diastolic, or with symptoms (poor feeding, sweating, cyanosis, faltering growth).
Cardiac auscultation video: Paediatric cardiac auscultation technique (YouTube)
▲ Murmur with pathological features or associated symptoms
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: urgent cardiology referral via NP. Specialist: Paediatric cardiologist.
▲ Persistent wheeze or recurrent lower respiratory tract infections
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: management review or specialist referral via NP. Specialist: Paediatric respiratory physician or general paediatrician.
18 months and older — gait evaluation
What to look forWhy it matters
Observe child walking across the room. Watch heel-strike, base of gait, arm swing, symmetry.Broad-based gait is normal at 12–18 months. Persistent toe-walking after 2 years, asymmetric gait, or in-toeing/out-toeing beyond expected correction warrants assessment.
If not walking at 18 months — explore in developmental assessment.Absent independent walking at 18 months is a developmental red flag requiring investigation.
▲ Not walking independently at 18 months
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: developmental paediatrics referral via NP. Specialist: Developmental paediatrician.
▲ Asymmetric gait, persistent limp, or pain on movement
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: paediatric or orthopaedic referral via NP. Specialist: Paediatric orthopaedic surgeon or general paediatrician.
▲ Persistent toe-walking past 2 years
Refer to NP. Typical referral path: physiotherapy assessment via NP referral; consider neurological cause. Specialist: Physiotherapist; neurological review if concerns persist.

Talking with families

How you communicate findings is as important as what you find. Your tone and language determine whether families engage with recommendations — or disengage entirely.

Starting the conversation
Suggested opening

"Before we go through the questionnaire, I'd love to hear from you — is there anything about [child's name]'s development that you've been thinking about or that you'd like us to look at today?"

Why this works

It gives the parent permission to voice concerns without feeling like they are criticising their child. It builds rapport before you move into structured assessment.

Sharing results — when things are on track
Example

"The questionnaire shows that [name]'s development is on track across all the areas we looked at today. That's great news. Is there anything you'd like more information about — like activities you can do at home to support her language development?"

Note

Even when results are positive, offer something actionable. It builds trust and demonstrates the value of the check beyond a simple "all clear".

Raising a concern sensitively
Example

"I noticed today that [name] isn't yet putting two words together — things like 'more juice' or 'daddy go'. At this age, we'd usually expect to hear that. It doesn't mean anything is wrong, but it is something we'd like to keep an eye on and get some extra support around. I'd like to refer you to a speech pathologist — they're the best people to help us understand what's going on and what to do next. How does that sound?"

Key principles

Name the specific observation. Normalise the referral. Invite the parent into the decision. Avoid the word "delayed" — use "not yet" language instead.

When a parent disagrees with your concern
Example

"That's really helpful context — you know [name] better than anyone. I'll make a note of what we've discussed today, and if you ever want to revisit it or have more questions, we're always here. I might just flag it as something to review at the next check if that's okay."

Always document: Record the conversation, the parent's response, and your clinical reasoning. If you recommended referral and the parent declined, document that too.

Safeguarding & resources

Developmental assessment and child protection are inseparable. Developmental delay, regression, or unusual behavioural presentations may be the first visible sign of abuse, neglect, or family adversity.

Development as a safeguarding lens
1
Significant delay with no medical explanation
Particularly with poor weight gain, poor hygiene, or infrequent medical contact. Consider neglect.
2
Regression following a disclosed or suspected abusive event
A child who was developing typically and then regresses after a change in family circumstances warrants careful attention.
3
Emotional or behavioural presentation inconsistent with age
Extreme withdrawal, aggression, hypersexualised behaviour, or dissociation in a young child requires careful assessment.
Mandatory reporting — South Australia

Under the SA Children and Young People (Safety) Act 2017, you are a mandatory notifier. You must notify CARL if you have a reasonable suspicion that a child is being abused, neglected, or exposed to family violence. You do not need proof. Your obligation is individual — you do not need management approval before contacting CARL.
